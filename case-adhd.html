Association Between ADHD Medication Use and Tobacco Use Behaviors

An independent cross-sectional analysis of NSDUH 2023
Type Independent research, EPID 671 (Psychiatric Epidemiology) Institution University of Michigan School of Public Health Advisor Briana Mezuk, PhD Timeline Nov 2025 – Apr 2026
Background

ADHD and substance use are known to co-occur, but most of the evidence is drawn from adolescent samples. I wanted to know whether the association holds at a population level across the full range of ages the disorder affects, and — more specifically — whether it reflects smoking initiation or persistence. That distinction matters: it points to a different place in the care pathway to intervene.

Data & Methods

I used the 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), a nationally representative, multistage probability sample fielded by SAMHSA. The analytic sample was derived from an initial 56,705 respondents down to a final N of 56,609 after excluding missing exposure and outcome records, weighted to represent roughly 283 million U.S. residents aged 12 and older.

  • Exposure: past-year ADHD prescription medication use, used as a proxy for clinically managed ADHD status.
  • Primary outcome: current smoking (past 30 days). Secondary outcome: nicotine dependence.
  • Design: full complex-survey specification in R (the survey package) — analysis weights, variance strata, and primary sampling units, with Taylor series linearization for variance estimation.
  • Modeling: survey-weighted logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and education.
  • Confounding control: a directed acyclic graph treating ADHD status, mental health, and socioeconomic status as unmeasured constructs, used to guide covariate selection and reason about the expected direction of exposure misclassification bias.
  • Analysis used a de-identified, public-use dataset exempt from further human subjects review.

The two groups being compared weren't demographically similar to begin with: people reporting ADHD medication use were significantly younger, more likely to be non-Hispanic White, and more likely to have some college education than non-users (all p<.001) — which is exactly why the adjustment set and the DAG-guided confounding control mattered for this analysis.

Weighted % of each group within each age category (Table 1). Categories 9–11 are the oldest age bands and hold 65% of the non-medication group versus 41% of the medication group — the medication group is concentrated in the younger categories instead.

Findings

Four model specifications were run against a pre-specified analysis plan: a main model, a secondary outcome, and two sensitivity analyses.

Before adjustment, current smoking is roughly 1.7× more common among people using ADHD medication than among those who aren't — the unadjusted picture behind the crude and adjusted odds ratios below.

The key read isn't the 2.08 — it's the drop to 1.46. Restricting the sample to people who have ever smoked attenuates the adjusted odds ratio from 2.08 to 1.46. That pattern suggests ADHD-related traits act more on smoking initiation than on persistence — consistent with prior longitudinal evidence that ADHD populations start smoking earlier and progress faster. Comparing model specifications, not just reporting a single effect size, is what surfaces that.
Limitations & Implications

The cross-sectional design can't establish temporal order or causation. Using medication as a proxy for ADHD status likely introduces non-differential misclassification, which typically biases estimates toward the null — meaning the true association may be somewhat larger than what's reported here. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors (for example, prenatal exposures) remains possible. Practically, the findings support integrating tobacco screening into routine ADHD clinical management, with particular attention to the adolescent window where initiation risk appears highest.
