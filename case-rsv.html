Mapping Hospital Adoption Barriers for an RSV Immunization Program

Market access research for a multinational pharmaceutical client
Role Medical Consulting Intern, BenHealth Consulting & Technology Location Shanghai Timeline Feb – Aug 2024
Background

A multinational pharmaceutical client needed to understand why hospital adoption of its RSV immunization program varied so widely across China's health system, ahead of a formulary-access push. The client name is withheld here in line with standard consulting confidentiality practice.

Methods

I designed the interview sampling frame myself, stratifying it across four institution types — tertiary pediatric departments, children's specialty hospitals, maternity hospitals, and community immunization sites — so that adoption barriers could be compared across institution types rather than pooled into a single undifferentiated sample. Working within a five-person engagement team, I then conducted 60+ structured interviews (roughly 30 minutes each) with physicians and hospital administrators, and owned the post-interview work of cleaning and structuring open-ended responses into a comparable analytical dataset.

Findings
  • Lower-tier hospitals often didn't recognize that year-round infant immunization was recommended — assuming, incorrectly, that only infants born in peak RSV season needed it. That's an awareness gap, not a pricing or channel problem.
  • Reimbursement status was the default decision rule in lower-tier markets: hospitals defaulted to whatever was already covered, rather than switching to a newly launched product.
  • Physicians consistently said reimbursement status strongly shaped the caregiver's purchase decision — since caregivers, not physicians, are the ones actually paying.
  • Safety, more than efficacy, was physicians' top prescribing consideration across almost every interview.
  • Tier-1 tertiary hospitals adopted fastest, and specifically in response to strong safety and efficacy data — the segment least constrained by cost and most responsive to clinical evidence.
The adoption barrier isn't one thing — it's a different thing at every layer. Tier-1 tertiary hospitals move on clinical evidence (safety data first, useful as an early-adoption and KOL anchor). Lower-tier hospitals need the immunization-awareness gap closed before pricing even becomes relevant. And reimbursement runs underneath both layers, because the real payer throughout is the caregiver, not the physician.
Implications

The layered structure of the barrier implies a layered sequencing of the client's evidence and messaging: lead with safety data in tier-1 markets to convert the most evidence-sensitive segment first and generate KOL support, while treating physician and caregiver education on immunization timing — not price — as the near-term lever in lower-tier markets. Reimbursement strategy needs to run underneath both, since it is the variable caregivers actually respond to.
